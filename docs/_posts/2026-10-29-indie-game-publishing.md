# Indie Game Publishing: Publisher or Self-Publish?

![Hand-drawn game publishing title card](https://media.babylovegrowth.ai/blog-images/organization-61610/1793211829481_Hand-drawn-game-publishing-title-card.jpeg)

Indie game publishing comes down to one decision: do you sign with a publisher who funds and launches your game, or do you release it yourself on Steam, itch.io or another store? Neither route is better in general. A publisher helps when you need money or lack time for marketing, QA and store work. Self-publishing keeps control and revenue, and costs you hours you would otherwise spend building. This guide covers both routes, how to pitch, what to check in a contract, and what a first self-release actually requires.

## TL;DR

- A publisher can bring funding, QA, localization, porting, marketing and community support. The exact package differs from one publisher to the next, so ask for it in writing.
- Self-publishing fits teams with marketing experience and the time to run launch work. You carry the money risk and every release task.
- Pitch roughly a year before launch with a playable build, a short deck, your budget and the specific support you want.
- In a contract, read recoupment, revenue splits, IP, termination and release obligations before anything else.
- On Steam, the app fee is $100 per app, and a first release has mandatory waiting periods. Plan your calendar around them.

## What a publisher actually does for an indie game

A publisher is a business partner that takes on part of the work of getting your game to players, and often part of the cost. Sweden's patent office describes publishers as companies that [publish and market games and invest in projects](https://www.prv.se/en/game-development/pitch-your-idea/the-role-of-the-publishers/), sometimes supporting financing, expertise, product development, distribution and advertising.

In practice the menu looks like this, according to [GameDev.net's Publishing 101 guide](https://gamedev.net/tutorials/business/business-and-law/publishing-101-should-you-partner-with-a-game-publisher-r5137): distribution, development advice, financing, porting, QA, talent, localization, media outreach, advertising, influencer outreach, trailers and community support. The same guide stresses that services vary by publisher.

Publishers describe their own offers in similar terms:

- [Shiro Games](https://shirogames.com/publishing/) lists financing, strategic marketing, digital distribution, business development, porting, playtests, QA and localization, so developers can focus more on development.
- [Jackbox Games](https://www.jackboxgames.com/games/publishing) says its partnerships typically include funding, production and release support, marketing, platform and certification guidance, QA, localization, porting and store management.
- Super Rare Games told 80.lv that its support can include [funding, PR, QA, milestones, storefront work, events and community management](https://80.lv/articles/insights-on-indie-game-publishing-from-super-rare-games).

The boring items matter as much as the glamorous ones. Mooneye Studios, a small developer, notes that a publisher can handle [age ratings, store preparation, platform registrations and development kits](https://www.mooneyestudios.com/news/lessonslearned/lessons-learned-self-publishing-vs-working-with-a-publisher/), all tasks that eat a solo developer's weeks.

## Publisher or self-publishing: how to decide

Start with what your team can execute reliably right now. A capability-first view, from [Funccards' comparison of the two routes](https://funccards.com/publisher-vs-self-publish/), says the choice should depend on what you can do well today, what you can learn in time, and which launch jobs would be too risky to learn during release. It is not a question of ideology.

### When a publisher makes sense

- You need money to finish. Funccards points to publisher value being strongest when a studio needs [serious production or user-acquisition funding](https://funccards.com/publisher-vs-self-publish/).
- You have no go-to-market experience. Mooneye says a publisher is especially useful when you [lack time to build a community or to learn QA, marketing, store pages and release processes](https://www.mooneyestudios.com/news/lessonslearned/lessons-learned-self-publishing-vs-working-with-a-publisher/).
- You want platform or regional reach you cannot build yourself, such as console ports or a localized launch.

### When self-publishing makes more sense

Game Developer's analysis says self-publishing [preserves freedom over what, why and when you develop](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/self-publishing-vs-working-with-a-traditional-publisher), but it makes you responsible for obtaining and managing the money needed to finish and launch. Hooded Horse's CEO Tim Bender argued in 2024, as [quoted by GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/city-builder/if-i-had-a-game-i-would-not-sign-with-90-of-publishers-manor-lords-publisher-thinks-self-publishing-is-a-fine-way-to-go/), that self-publishing can be sensible if you are willing to learn marketing, because you avoid publisher costs and the risk of your game being mis-positioned.

Self-publishing means you own the whole list. The Game Marketer's guide spells it out: [platform uploads, marketing, community campaigns, press and influencer outreach, updates, bug reports and user support](https://www.thegamemarketer.com/insight-posts/choosing-the-right-path-for-your-game-self-publishing-partnering-or-publisher).

### A quick self-check

| Question | Leans publisher | Leans self-publish |
|---|---|---|
| Can you fund the last 6 to 12 months of development? | No | Yes |
| Have you shipped and marketed a game before? | No | Yes |
| Do you already have a wishlist base or community? | No | Yes |
| Do you need console ports or many languages at launch? | Yes | Not at launch |
| Is full creative and IP control non-negotiable? | Negotiate hard | Yes |

There is no scoring rule here. If three or more rows pull the same way, that is your default, and you can test it against real publisher feedback.

## How to find publishers that fit your game

Do not limit your list to famous names. Game Developer's piece on pitching advises [asking indie peers for introductions, using lists and groups, and considering regional publishers](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/always-be-pitching-how-to-find-and-talk-to-publishers-to-survive-as-an-indie). Check that a publisher's portfolio and platform connections match your game.

Game Scout's guide lists [conferences, agencies, direct outreach and online platforms](https://gamescout.biz/blog/five-ways-of-finding-a-publisher-for-your-video-game) as routes. Before you build a shortlist, it advises defining the funding you want, your IP position, the services you need, the communication style you prefer and the working relationship you expect.

Then do the homework most developers skip: look at what a publisher has actually released. Tim Bender's view, reported by [Creative Bloq in 2026](https://www.creativebloq.com/3d/video-game-design/do-game-devs-need-to-avoid-predatory-and-opportunistic-indie-publishers), is that many publishers add limited value while keeping recoupment rights, so check their release track record. Practical steps:

1. Open the publisher's catalog and note which games launched in the last three years.
2. Check whether those games match your genre and price band.
3. Message two or three of their developers and ask what the publisher did after signing.
4. Drop anyone who cannot name specific marketing and QA commitments.

## How to pitch a publisher

Timing matters. Tim from Hooded Horse recommends in [GameDev.tv's guide](https://gamedev.tv/articles/how-to-get-your-game-published-with-guest-tim-from-hooded-horse) approaching publishers about a year before launch, and says a clear unique selling point and genre fit improve a pitch. A year out leaves time to discuss the vision, resolve rough patches and plan the release. Pitching when launch is imminent weakens your position.

### What goes in the pitch

GamesBeat's pitching tips from publishers say a submission should include a [high-level premise, team size and experience, development stage, target platforms and release window, gameplay footage from a prototype or vertical slice, requested support, a playable build and a proposed budget](https://gamesbeat.com/the-indiebeat-tips-from-publishers-and-how-to-pitch-to-them/).

Publishers' own forms confirm the pattern:

- [Raw Fury](https://rawfury.com/how-to-pitch-to-raw-fury/) asks for a playable PC build, a development budget and a deck covering what you are building, why, who is building it, where you want to go, what you need and when it will arrive. GIFs, videos, a design document or concept art can supplement it.
- [Shiro Games](https://shirogames.com/publishing/) asks for an elevator pitch, genres, total project amount, required financing, support needs, a pitch deck, videos or screenshots and a Steam URL. Its deck guidance covers the game overview, core gameplay loop, production timeline and budget, team and art style.

### A pitch that gets read

Lead with the loop, not the lore. Show 60 seconds of real gameplay. State your budget as a number with a breakdown, and say exactly what you want from the publisher: money, QA, ports, marketing, or all of it. Vague asks read as inexperience.

## Reading a publishing agreement

The contract decides who gets paid and who controls the game. Sweden's PRV warns that publishers may seek [copyright transfer, sequel options, creative and marketing control, and all revenue until costs are recouped](https://www.prv.se/en/game-development/pitch-your-idea/the-role-of-the-publishers/). These are negotiation points, not universal rules.

Winkler Partners' checklist tells you what to look at: [advances and recoupable costs, pre- and post-recoupment splits, physical, digital and merchandise revenue, control of distribution and seller accounts, advertising approval, IP ownership and licenses, territory, platforms and local-law responsibilities](https://winklerpartners.com/5-things-to-note-in-a-video-game-publishing-agreement/). Their warning is worth underlining: an attractive split can still be delayed by vague deductions or recoupment rules, so the language must be operationally precise.

![Reading a publishing agreement — overview diagram](https://media.babylovegrowth.ai/blog-images/organization-61610/1793211886174_Reading-a-publishing-agreement-overview-diagram.jpeg)

Games lawyer Felix Wong, interviewed by [PreMortem Games](https://premortem.games/2023/05/30/navigating-publishing-agreements-insights-from-games-lawyer-felix-wong/), suggests scrutinizing revenue share, deductions, recoupment, how subjective milestones are, termination, non-compete clauses, ancillary rights and whether the publisher is contractually obliged to release and market the game. His point about relationships is simple: write specific, testable commitments instead of relying on informal promises about marketing, approvals, release or support.

### Questions to put to the publisher

- What costs are recoupable, and at what share of revenue are they recouped?
- Who holds the Steam and console seller accounts?
- What happens to the rights if the publisher misses a release date?
- Do I keep the IP, and what are the sequel and merchandise terms?
- Which marketing actions are guaranteed in the contract, not just discussed?

Before you sign, have a lawyer who works in games read the agreement. A general business lawyer may not know what recoupment or platform account control means in practice.

## What self-publishing involves in practice

Self-publishing is mostly logistics. Here is the Steam path, because it is the most common first stop for PC games.

### Steam: fees and timing

Steam is open to developers worldwide. You complete paperwork, pay the app fee, provide bank, tax and identity information, prepare your store page and build, and pass review, according to [Valve's Steam Direct page](https://partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect). The same page states that first releases require a 30-day wait after paying the app fee and a publicly visible Coming Soon page for at least two weeks, and that review typically takes one to five days.

The fee is [$100 per app, is not refundable, and becomes recoupable after the product reaches $1,000 in adjusted gross revenue](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee) from Steam Store and in-app purchases, per Valve's documentation.

![Steam Direct fees, timing, and recoupment threshold](https://media.babylovegrowth.ai/blog-images/organization-61610/1793211863105_Steam-Direct-fees-timing-and-recoupment-threshold.jpeg)

Work backward from those rules. If you want a launch on a specific week, pay the fee and publish your Coming Soon page well ahead, then use that page to collect wishlists while you finish the build.

### Other routes

Mobile means submitting through Apple's and Google's stores, each with its own metadata, age-rating, privacy and review requirements. Apple's [App Store submission guidance](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/submitting/) covers product-page metadata, age-rating and privacy information, and TestFlight for beta testing. Browser and download releases on itch.io are lighter weight and suit prototypes, jam games and small commercial titles. Our own [archive of itch.io's early posts on running an indie game store](https://www.itch.io/blog/1/running-an-indie-game-store/) shows how that platform thinks about creator-first selling.

### A self-publishing checklist

1. Set a launch window and work backward from store waiting periods.
2. Build a store page with a trailer, clear screenshots and a one-line hook.
3. Start collecting wishlists or followers months before release.
4. Run a closed playtest and fix the top crashes.
5. Prepare a press kit and contact creators who cover your genre.
6. Plan the first month of patches and support before you click publish.

## Where RPG Maker developers fit in

If you build with RPG Maker MV or MZ, you are in a good spot for the self-publishing route, because the engine keeps production costs low and scope manageable. Most small RPG Maker teams we talk to are one to three people with limited budgets, which is exactly the profile that has to decide whether a publisher's money is worth the revenue share.

The practical advice is the same as for any engine. Cut scope so you can finish. Build a vertical slice early, since it is the thing publishers and players both want to see. Then put your remaining effort where the game stands out: systems, art direction, and polish players notice in the first ten minutes.

## FAQ

### Do I need a publisher to release an indie game?

No. Steam is open to developers worldwide, and you can release on it yourself after completing the paperwork, paying the app fee and passing review. A publisher is an option when you need funding or lack time for marketing, QA and store work.

### How much does it cost to self-publish on Steam?

The Steam Direct fee is $100 per app. It is not refundable, but it becomes recoupable once the product reaches $1,000 in adjusted gross revenue on Steam. Your real budget also includes trailer, QA, localization and marketing, which the fee does not cover.

### When should I pitch a publisher?

About a year before your planned launch, according to Hooded Horse's Tim. You should have a playable build or vertical slice, a budget, and a clear statement of the support you want. Pitching late weakens your negotiating position.

### What is recoupment in a publishing deal?

Recoupment is the publisher recovering its advance and other costs from your game's revenue before you receive your normal share. The key points to check are which costs count, what share of revenue goes to recoupment, and which deductions apply. Ambiguous wording can delay your payouts even if the headline split looks fair.
