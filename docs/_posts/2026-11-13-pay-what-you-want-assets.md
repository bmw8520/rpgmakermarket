## What pay what you want means for game assets

Pay what you want is a pricing model where the buyer chooses the amount instead of paying a fixed price. [Sellfy describes it](https://sellfy.com/blog/pay-what-you-want/) as a setup in which the seller can set a minimum, so buyers can't always type in any amount, and can also show a suggested price as a hint.

Two terms matter when you shop:

- **Minimum price:** the lowest amount you may enter. [FairFate's docs](https://fairfate.com/docs/pricing-models) define it as the lowest amount a customer may enter.
- **Suggested price:** the amount the seller recommends. The same docs note that it acts as a reference point and has to respect any minimum.

PWYW is not the same thing as free. [Sellfy's explanation](https://sellfy.com/blog/pay-what-you-want/) is that with PWYW the seller sets a minimum, and the buyer decides whether to pay that minimum or more. On itch.io specifically, a seller can leave the floor at zero or set a minimum, according to [IndiePilot's guide](https://indiepilot.dev/guides/itch-io-pay-what-you-want/). That guide also notes that pages can be built around reward tiers, so paying above a threshold unlocks extra content.

Why do sellers choose it? [CheckoutPage's guide](https://checkoutpage.com/blog/pay-what-you-want-pricing) says PWYW suits products where each extra sale costs little to fulfill, such as PDFs and templates. Digital game assets fit that description well. That's why you'll find so many PWYW packs: the download costs the creator almost nothing to deliver, so a flexible price works for both sides.

## What to check before you use any asset

A low price doesn't settle whether you can ship the game. Run through these before you import anything.

1. **Commercial use.** Can you sell a game that contains the asset? Some licenses allow it; others don't. Itch.io even has browse pages by license, such as assets under CC BY-SA 4.0 and assets under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The NC in the second name stands for non-commercial, which matters if you plan to charge for your game.
2. **Attribution.** Does the seller want credit in your game? Add a credits screen from the start so it's never an afterthought.
3. **Redistribution.** You can usually use an asset inside your game, but you may not be allowed to repackage the raw files and give or sell them on.
4. **Engine ownership.** Some libraries require you to own MV or MZ, as the Hiddenone terms do.
5. **Version support.** Confirm the asset says MV, MZ or both. A plugin made for one engine version won't necessarily run in the other.
6. **Update and support terms.** Look at the product page for how long the seller keeps updating and where you can ask questions.

If you plan to build products for resale or client work, whitelabel-ready resources save you the license guesswork. That's one reason we offer them.

## How to judge quality before you spend

PWYW lowers the cost of trying something, but your time is still the real cost. A fast way to judge an asset:

- **Look at the page.** Clear screenshots, a description of what's included and a list of supported engine versions all signal that the seller cares about buyers.
- **Check file formats.** For audio, loop-ready files save editing work. For art, check that tilesheets match RPG Maker's grid sizes so they slot straight into the editor.
- **Read the update history.** A product that keeps getting updates is easier to live with when the engine changes.
- **Match the asset to the job.** A quick classroom game needs a ready minigame, not a 40-file art pack. A small studio expanding an RPG needs a plugin that integrates cleanly.

## Choosing a price when you pay

Once you've found a pack, the price box is the last decision. A few practical habits:

- **Pay at least the suggested amount if the asset saves you real hours.** The suggested price is the seller's reference point, and the seller sets any minimum.
- **Pay more for tools you'll reuse.** A plugin you'll use in five games earns more of your money than a one-off tile.
- **Look for reward tiers.** On pages structured around them, paying above a threshold can unlock extra content, per [IndiePilot's itch.io guide](https://indiepilot.dev/guides/itch-io-pay-what-you-want/).
- **Budget for the whole project.** If you'll need many plugins, a one-off pass can be simpler than pricing each download.

Creators who run PWYW often depend on buyers paying fairly, and the money funds further updates. That's a good enough reason to pay something sensible when a pack earns its place in your project.

## FAQ

### Is pay what you want the same as free?

No. With PWYW the seller can set a minimum price, and you decide whether to pay that minimum or more, as [Sellfy explains](https://sellfy.com/blog/pay-what-you-want/). On itch.io, a seller may also leave the minimum at zero. Either way, the seller chooses the floor and a suggested amount.

### Can I use pay-what-you-want assets in a commercial game?

It depends on the license attached to each asset. Some are public domain, like the Open Game Art Bundle, which [became CC0](https://opensource.com/life/13/7/download-open-game-art-bundle). Others restrict commercial use. Read the terms on the product page before you ship.

### Do I need to own RPG Maker to use these assets?

For engine-tied libraries, often yes. Hiddenone's Resource Warehouse, for example, [requires you to own](https://www.hiddenone-sprites.com/terms-of-use.html) the MV or MZ license. Our plugins and minigames are designed for MV and MZ, so you'll want the engine anyway.

### What should I pay for a PWYW asset?

Start from the suggested price, which is the seller's recommended amount. If the asset saves you hours or you'll reuse it across projects, paying more is a fair way to support updates. You can't go below any minimum the seller has set.

### Is a one-off pass better than paying per asset?

If you'll use many plugins across several games, a single payment is easier to manage. The Lifetime Supporter Pass is 79.99 USD one-off, listed on [our pass page](https://rpg-maker-plugins.itch.io/lifetime-supporter-pass/).
